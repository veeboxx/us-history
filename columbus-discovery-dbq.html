1. Who Was Christopher Columbus?
Core Text

Christopher Columbus was born in the Republic of Genoa, in present-day Italy, in 1451, and went to sea as a teenager. Convinced that a westward route across the Atlantic Ocean would be a faster path to the wealth of Asia than the long overland routes controlled by Muslim traders, he spent nearly a decade seeking a monarch willing to fund the voyage. Portugal rejected him twice. It was Spain's King Ferdinand and Queen Isabella who finally agreed in 1492, shortly after completing their own conquest of Granada.

On August 3, 1492, Columbus set sail from the port of Palos with three ships — the Niña, the Pinta, and the Santa María. After thirty-six days at sea, he and his crew reached an island in the present-day Bahamas, which he claimed for Spain. The TaínoThe Taíno were the Indigenous people who lived on the island Columbus landed on in 1492, and across much of the Caribbean. people who already lived there greeted the sailors and traded goods with them, including cotton and small amounts of gold jewelry.

Columbus made four voyages to the Americas between 1492 and 1504. Believing he had reached the outer edge of Asia, he called the people he encountered "Indians" — a mistaken label that stuck for centuries. On Hispaniola, the island now shared by Haiti and the Dominican Republic, Columbus established a forced labor policyA system requiring people to work against their will, often under threat of punishment. over the Taíno population in search of gold, despite Queen Isabella's own objections to slavery. In 1500, a royal investigator arrested Columbus for his brutal and chaotic governing of the colony and sent him back to Spain in chains. He died in 1506, still believing he had found a new route to Asia.

2. The Case for Celebrating Columbus
Core Text

Supporters of Columbus Day argue that Columbus should be understood within the context of his own era rather than judged entirely by today's standards. Nearly every civilization in world history, they point out — including the Aztec, Maya, and Inca empires that Spanish conquistadors later encountered — practiced warfare, conquest, and even human sacrifice long before 1492. Some historians argue that holding one individual personally responsible for the deaths caused by disease, which spread gradually over decades and which no one at the time understood scientifically, oversimplifies a far more complicated history.

Beyond the historical debate, supporters also point to Columbus's undeniable impact and symbolism. His voyage connected two hemispheres that had developed separately for thousands of years, launching an exchange of crops, animals, and ideas that reshaped the modern world. Columbus also holds deep cultural meaning for many Italian-American and Hispanic-American communities, who have celebrated Columbus Day with parades for generations as a way of claiming their own place in American history, especially after facing discrimination themselves in the late 1800s and early 1900s.

3. The Case Against Celebrating Columbus
Core Text

Critics argue that celebrating Columbus overlooks the direct and devastating consequences of his actions. Columbus personally established the forced labor system on Hispaniola, and colonists under his command used violence to control the Taíno population and extract gold. Within a few decades of Columbus's arrival, the Taíno population of Hispaniola had collapsed catastrophically due to a combination of forced labor, violence, and disease — one of the earliest chapters in a much longer history of harm done to Indigenous peoples across the Americas.

Critics also argue that treating Columbus Day and Indigenous Peoples Day as an either-or choice misses the larger point: that American history includes both remarkable achievement and serious injustice, and a full account should hold space for both rather than erasing one to protect the other. Some cities, including Los Angeles, have replaced Columbus Day with Indigenous Peoples Day specifically to center the experiences of Native peoples that traditional celebrations often left out entirely.

Primary Source: Bartolomé de las Casas, on Spanish treatment of the Taíno (1552)
"Of all the infinite universe of humanity, these people are the most guileless, the most devoid of wickedness... Yet into this sheepfold... there came some Spaniards who immediately behaved like ravening wild beasts."
