The Admiral Who Got It Wrong

He crossed an ocean nobody in Europe had crossed before. He never figured out what he'd actually found.

Age of Exploration 8 min read

On the night of October 11, 1492, a sailor named Rodrigo de Triana was standing watch on the Pinta when he saw something in the dark that did not belong there: land. He shouted down to the deck. Within hours, three ships that had been at sea for more than nine weeks dropped anchor off a small island in the Bahamas. Christopher Columbus had crossed the Atlantic Ocean, and as far as he was concerned, he had just reached the outer edge of Asia.

He was wrong about that. He would stay wrong about it for the rest of his life.

Columbus had spent close to a decade trying to convince someone, anyone, to fund a westward voyage to the riches of the East Indies — the spices, silks, and gold that European traders normally reached by long, expensive overland routes. Portugal turned him down twice. France and England were not interested. It was Spain's King Ferdinand and Queen Isabella who finally agreed, fresh off their conquest of Granada and looking for a new way to compete with Portugal's sea routes around Africa. Columbus sailed from the port of Palos on August 3, 1492, with three ships — the Niña, the Pinta, and the Santa María — and a crew that grew more nervous with every week that passed without sighting land.

When land finally appeared, Columbus named the island San Salvador and claimed it for Spain. The people who already lived there, the Taíno, had their own name for it: Guanahaní. Columbus never asked what they called themselves, either. Because he believed he had landed near the East Indies, he called them IndiansA term Columbus used because he believed he had reached the East Indies. It became — incorrectly — the common European name for Native peoples across the Americas., a label that stuck for the next five hundred years despite being based on a geography error.

In his journal, Columbus described the Taíno as gentle, generous, and easy to overpower — noting that with just a small number of armed men, he believed he could conquer the entire population. Paraphrased from his October 1492 journal entries, as preserved by Bartolomé de las Casas

That single passage says more about what came next than almost anything else in the historical record. Columbus made four voyages across the Atlantic between 1492 and 1504, and each one pushed further into conquest and less into exploration. He was appointed governor of the Spanish colonies he established on the island of Hispaniola, and his rule there was, by the accounts of his own contemporaries, a disaster. Forced labor, brutal punishments, and unchecked violence against the Taíno population led to a wave of complaints reaching the Spanish crown. In 1500, a royal investigator named Francisco de Bobadilla arrived, arrested Columbus, and sent him back to Spain in chains.

The Man Who Named a World He'd Never Seen

Columbus was eventually released and even allowed to sail one more time, but he never regained his titles. He died in 1506, still insisting that the islands he had explored were part of Asia — not a separate, previously unknown-to-Europeans continent. He never once set foot on the mainland of North America. His voyages stayed almost entirely in the Caribbean and, later, the coasts of Central and South America.

The final irony took only a few more years to arrive. A mapmaker printing a new world atlas needed a name for the continents Columbus had brushed against but never understood. He chose to honor a different Italian explorer, Amerigo Vespucci, who had argued — correctly, unlike Columbus — that this was a previously unknown landmass entirely. The name that stuck to two continents was America. Not Columbus.

Think About It

Columbus is often taught as the man who "discovered America," even though he never reached the mainland, misidentified where he was for the rest of his life, and the continents were ultimately named after someone else. Why do you think his name became the one most associated with 1492? What gets remembered about history, and what gets left out?

Millions of people already lived across the Americas when Columbus arrived — societies with their own governments, trade networks, cities, and histories stretching back thousands of years. What Columbus's voyage actually started wasn't a discovery. It was a collision between two hemispheres that had developed separately for thousands of years, and it would reshape both of them permanently, for better and for very much worse.
