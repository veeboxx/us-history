The Fountain That Wasn't

He's remembered for chasing eternal youth. He was actually chasing gold, land, and a rumor — and none of it saved him.

Age of Exploration 7 min read

Every spring, textbooks and trivia nights repeat the same story: a Spanish explorer named Juan Ponce de León sailed to Florida in search of a magical fountain that could restore youth to anyone who drank from it. It is one of the most famous legends in American history. It is also, as far as historians can tell, almost entirely made up — and invented by someone who did not even like him very much.

Ponce de León was a real person with a real, well-documented career, and none of the surviving records from his own lifetime mention a fountain of anything. He had sailed with Columbus on his second voyage back in 1493, fought in Spain's campaigns against the Taíno on Hispaniola, and eventually became the first Spanish governor of Puerto Rico. By 1513, he was wealthy, well-connected, and looking for a new opportunity. Rumors were circulating about an island called Bimini somewhere north of the Caribbean, supposedly rich in gold. Ponce de León outfitted three ships and went looking for it.

He never found Bimini. What he found instead, in April of 1513, was a coastline nobody in Europe had charted before. He came ashore somewhere along Florida's Atlantic coast — historians still debate exactly where — during the Spanish Easter season known as Pascua FloridaSpanish for "Flowery Easter," the feast-day season during which Ponce de León landed — the likely source of the name he gave the land: La Florida., the feast of flowers. He named the land La Florida in honor of it. As far as written records show, he was the first European to set foot on what is now the continental United States.

Where the Legend Actually Came From

So where did the fountain of youth come from? Not from Ponce de León's own writing, which does not survive in any detail describing his motivations that way. The story appears to trace back to a Spanish royal chronicler named Gonzalo Fernández de Oviedo, who published an account of the Indies in the 1530s — years after Ponce de León had died. Oviedo's version of events was not exactly flattering. He mocked Ponce de León as an old man foolishly obsessed with restoring his youth and his romantic vigor. Whether Oviedo was reporting a real rumor or inventing one to make his subject look ridiculous is something historians still argue about. Either way, the story caught on. Five hundred years later, it is nearly the only thing most people know about him.

The documented goals of the 1513 expedition were gold, land, and enslaved labor for Puerto Rico's colony — a far less magical, far more familiar set of motives for a Spanish expedition of the era. Consensus of modern historical scholarship on the 1513 voyage

The real story, without the fountain, is arguably more interesting anyway. Ponce de León returned to Florida in 1521, eight years after his first landing, with two ships and roughly two hundred people, intending to build a permanent colony on the Gulf Coast near present-day Charlotte Harbor. The land was already home to the Calusa, a powerful and organized Native nation that had no interest in a Spanish settlement on their territory. They attacked. Ponce de León was struck by an arrow, likely poisoned, during the fighting. His men evacuated the wounded expedition to Spanish Cuba, where he died from the injury within weeks.

Think About It

The fountain of youth legend has outlasted almost everything true about Ponce de León's actual life. Why do you think a colorful myth can end up more famous than the real historical record? Can you think of other historical figures where the popular story and the documented history don't quite match?

He never found the fountain, because as far as the evidence shows, he was never really looking for one. He never found lasting wealth, either — the 1521 colony collapsed within weeks of his death. What he did find, whether he meant to or not, was the coastline that would eventually draw Spanish, French, and English colonizers for the next two centuries, each one chasing something they never quite found either.

Sources: Douglas T. Peck, Ponce de León and the Discovery of Florida (1993); National Park Service, De Soto National Memorial historical records; Encyclopedia of Florida History and Culture.
